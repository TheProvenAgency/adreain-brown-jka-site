The $25 Marketing Lesson My Son Taught Me

AB Adreain Brown · JKA Consulting · Marketing & Mindset

Short answer: consistency, not a perfect launch, is what turns a webinar funnel on — and sometimes the sharpest pricing instinct in the room belongs to a nine-year-old.

Two Webinars That Made Nothing

The first webinar made nothing. Not a little — nothing. The second one, the following week, made nothing again. It would have been easy to read that as a sign to quit or pivot back to funding only. Instead, the plan stayed the same for a third run.

That third webinar landed just days after losing a grandfather. Showing up anyway, still processing the loss, still getting on camera and teaching — that's the one that broke through. From there, the results started compounding week over week. The lesson wasn't a better hook or a better slide deck. It was staying in the seat through two dead launches to get to the one that worked.

The Question In The Car

Weeks later, during a week spent in Ohio with his son, a car ride turned into an unplanned business lesson. His son asked a simple question: people pay you to teach them, but what if someone doesn't have the money?

That question became a pricing exercise. If a five-day mentorship were built to teach exactly what his son had just asked about, what should it cost? His son's answer landed at $26. The links that already existed were built around a $25 price point, so $25 is what stuck — a number set almost entirely by a kid's gut instinct rather than a pricing spreadsheet.

The Almost-Mistake

The next test came fast. After a smaller launch sold at $25, the instinct was to raise the price for the next round and capture more revenue from the same audience. His son pushed back hard: "Dad, I don't want to destroy the business."

That single sentence held. No new links were created, no price increase went out. The next round sold roughly 40 people in at $25, and the round after that filled a room of 125 people for the challenge kickoff — proof that the honesty and consistency of the offer mattered more than squeezing extra dollars out of it.

Choosing Trust Over The Bigger Number

As the challenge grew, a business partner who'd invested heavily pushed for a bigger goal — 300 people, maximum revenue from the launch. The instinct that won out instead was smaller and slower: fill 50 spots, make sure every single person who paid got taken care of, and let the trust built from that launch fund the next one.

That decision came with a shift in posture that carried into every launch afterward — treating income as something that follows impact, not something chased ahead of it. Not selling for the sake of selling, but building something people could see was honest, then trusting that the paychecks would follow the people who needed the information.

Two flopped webinars, a grieving week that turned into the breakthrough, and a $25 price point set by a nine-year-old — none of it was a formula. It was showing up through the parts that didn't work, and letting people closest to the mission (including a kid in the passenger seat) shape the plan.
