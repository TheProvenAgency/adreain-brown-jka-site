Mentorship Is The Fast Pass: What $45K In Coaching Taught Me

AB Adreain Brown · JKA Consulting · Mindset & Mentorship

Short answer: not every mentorship pays off, and that's not a reason to stop investing in one — it's a reason to get better at vetting who you invest in, then commit fully once you do.

The Investments That Didn't Work

Early on, a $15,000 investment in a funding mentor produced nothing usable — a hard lesson in vetting before paying. A separate mentorship, one focused on digital marketing bought during an eight-hour, $15,000 VIP day in Atlanta, delivered real information — but it took months to fully grasp because the material wasn't retained well the first time. Neither loss was really about the mentor. Both were about not yet knowing how to evaluate fit or how to actually absorb what was being taught.

The One That Changed Everything

Two mentorships purchased the same week — on Black Friday, while still sleeping on a cousin's couch and averaging $30,000 a month — finally connected the dots between funding knowledge and an actual scalable business. One focused on credit repair depth, the other on the webinar and client-acquisition systems needed to turn that knowledge into consistent revenue. The first webinar built from that mentorship made nothing. The second made nothing. The third — run just days after a personal loss — converted, and consistency took over from there.

The Real Lesson

Mentors are described as "the life hack" — the fast pass that skips years of trial and error. But the fast pass only works if you actually get on the ride: implement what you're taught immediately, ask for clarity when something doesn't land instead of quietly giving up on it, and stop treating the investment as optional once you've made it.

"Stop being cheap trying to get rich. It's not going to work."

The pattern across every mentorship — the ones that worked and the ones that didn't — is the same: what you invest in yourself is what you attract in clients and outcomes. Holding back on that investment doesn't protect you from risk. It just slows down how fast you find out what actually works.

How To Actually Vet A Mentor Before Paying

The $15,000 that produced nothing wasn't a scam in the legal sense — it was a mismatch that better vetting would have caught. Before paying for any mentorship now, the questions are simple: can this person show real, verifiable results, not just screenshots? Do they teach the actual mechanics, or just the highlight reel? Is there a clear structure to what gets taught, or is it going to be vague calls that never quite get to the how? A mentor worth $15,000 should be able to answer those questions without getting defensive.

Why The Same Two Mentorships Bought The Same Week Worked

What made the Black Friday purchases different wasn't the price or even the timing — it was that they were bought to solve two specific, named gaps: credit repair depth on one side, and the webinar and client-acquisition systems needed to turn that knowledge into consistent revenue on the other. Neither purchase was "I hope this helps somehow." Both were targeted at a problem that had already been identified through the failures that came before them. That specificity is what separates a mentorship that pays for itself from one that just sits there.

What "Implementing Immediately" Actually Looks Like

It's easy to say implement what you're taught right away. In practice, that meant running the first webinar within days of the mentorship ending, not weeks — even though it made nothing. Then running the second one on schedule, even after the first flopped. Then running the third, on schedule, through active grief. The lesson wasn't in any single webinar. It was in refusing to let two failed launches change the plan before the plan had actually been given a fair test.

The Cost Of Not Investing At All

The flip side of a mentorship that doesn't hit isn't "don't invest" — it's "invest smarter next time." Staying cheap out of fear of another $15,000 loss doesn't protect anyone from years of trial and error; it just guarantees the trial and error happens slower and alone. Every mentor who's ever moved the needle here cost real money. The ones that didn't work still taught something: how to evaluate the next one better.

The material is the same caliber of what got paid thousands for in mentorship rooms; it's just been organized so a client doesn't have to guess whether it's real before committing.

The Part That Doesn't Show Up In A Highlight Reel

The third webinar — the one that finally converted — ran days after losing his grandfather, a man who'd told him plainly not to cry at his funeral. That same period of loss stretched into a harder run: a move to Florida where accounts froze and people he'd counted on disappeared inside of 45 days. None of the mentorships bought before or after that stretch changed what was happening in that season. What they gave him was a framework to keep executing through it, which is ultimately what turned around into a $347,000 month once the dust settled. A mentor can hand you the fast pass. Nobody hands you the will to still show up on camera three weeks after burying someone.

Fast Pass Doesn't Mean Easy Pass

It's worth being direct about what "fast pass" means here, because it's easy to misread. A mentor skips years of trial and error on strategy — which bank to call, how to price an offer, how to structure a webinar funnel. It does not skip the personal work of actually executing that strategy when a launch flops twice in a row, or when life is actively falling apart in the background. The mentorship gets you the map. Walking it is still on you.
