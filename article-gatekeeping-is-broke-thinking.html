Gatekeeping Is Broke Thinking

AB Adreain Brown · JKA Consulting · Mindset & Mentorship

Short answer: withholding information out of fear that someone else will "take your spot" is a scarcity mindset, not a business strategy — because information alone was never the hard part. Execution is.

Why Gatekeeping Happens

In the funding and credit space, plenty of people build a following without ever explaining how the strategies actually work. The instinct behind that is what gets called a poor man's mentality — the belief that teaching someone the full process hands them your position. The truth runs the other way: everybody's spot is for the taking regardless of how much information gets shared, because information was never the scarce resource.

The Game Is Free, The Execution Isn't

Every framework, every step of the funding sequence, every part of the process gets explained openly — on YouTube, on Instagram, in the free content, not just behind a paywall. That openness doesn't create competition, because most people who hear a strategy still won't act on it. Most people won't put in the work. Most people won't execute. Giving away the "how" doesn't threaten the business; it filters for the small number of people who'll actually do something with it.

An Analogy Worth Sitting With

The comparison drawn here is a faith-based one: teaching wasn't gatekept in scripture, and withholding it wouldn't have made the message more valuable — it would have made the outcome worse for everyone who needed it. Applied to a business built on funding education, the same logic holds. Somebody out there has nothing but time and opportunity, and the information they need to change their situation shouldn't be locked behind unnecessary walls just to protect a competitive edge that execution alone already protects.

Gatekeepers Don't Last

There's also a practical prediction built into this philosophy: people who gatekeep information out of insecurity tend not to last, because the motive shows. Being in it purely to protect a position instead of to actually help people is, eventually, visible to the audience — and it erodes trust faster than sharing "too much" ever could.

What Comes After Almost A Decade Of This

Zooming out from the day-to-day, the five-year outlook isn't about scaling the funding business bigger — it's about a shift in focus entirely. The expectation is a transition, arriving somewhere around age 33, toward a more ministry-oriented calling: less time behind a screen pushing applications through, more time directly in front of people. The target is full retirement from the day-to-day business by 35, timed deliberately so the last two years of his son's high school years are spent fully present rather than split between fatherhood and the business.

That's the same logic extended forward: the foundation being built now — funded, taught, given away in the open — is what buys back the time later. Not gatekeeping the information today is what makes stepping back from the business tomorrow possible, instead of being needed at the center of it forever.

"The game is free. The execution is what's not."

Gatekeeping protects an ego for a while. It doesn't protect a business, and it doesn't build the kind of trust that lets someone eventually step back and hand the work to the people they taught.

What This Looks Like In Practice, Not Just Theory

This isn't an abstract philosophy — it shows up as actual content. The bank-tier breakdowns, the credit card rankings, the full $50K in 48 Hours framework laid out step by step on this site: none of it is trimmed down or held back for a paid tier. The same is true on Instagram, where entire strategies get posted as reels for free, comment sections included, rather than teased behind a "DM me for the info" wall. If the strategy itself was the moat, none of this would be postable. It isn't the moat. Execution is.

Calling Out What Doesn't Match This

Part of building in the open is being willing to name what the alternative looks like. Content warning against "mentorship scams" and course-sellers who take payment and disappear isn't incidental — it's the same principle in reverse. If information is genuinely not the scarce resource, then charging premium prices for vague, gatekept access while delivering little of substance is the exact behavior this philosophy pushes back against. Buying a course and not doing the work won't get anyone results either way — but at minimum, the work being taught should be real.

Why Retirement By 35 Isn't A Contradiction

It might sound strange for someone still building a funding business to talk openly about stepping back from it in a matter of years. But that's the point: a business built on gatekept, personality-dependent information can't be stepped away from, because the founder is the product. A business built on taught, transferable systems — the same systems given away in public content — can eventually run without the founder standing in the middle of every transaction. Teaching everything openly now is what makes handing it off later structurally possible, not just emotionally desired.

The Bet Underneath All Of This

Gatekeeping is, at its core, a bet that scarcity protects you. This philosophy is the opposite bet: that generosity with information, paired with a business built on real results, compounds into more trust, more referrals, and more staying power than hoarding ever could. Almost a decade into this industry, that bet is still the one being made, in public, on purpose.
